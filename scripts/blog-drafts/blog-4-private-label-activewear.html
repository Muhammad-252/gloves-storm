Launching a fitness brand used to mean ordering 500 to 1,000 pieces per colour from a large factory. What is private label activewear?

Private label activewear is clothing made by a manufacturer and sold under your own brand name. You choose the product, fabric, colours and branding, and the factory handles pattern, cutting, stitching and finishing. There are three common models:

Model What you get Typical MOQ Best for
Relabel (white label) An existing garment with your label and tags Lowest Testing a market quickly
Semi-custom The factory's proven pattern with your fabric, colours and logo 30–50 pieces per style Most first collections
Full custom (cut and sew) Your own pattern, fit and construction 50+ pieces per style Brands with a distinct fit

* MOQs are approximate and depend on the garment. Confirm yours in writing before you plan a budget.

Fabric guide: nylon vs polyester blends

Fabric chemistry decides how activewear feels, how long it lasts, and how it looks after washing. When specifying materials with your factory, consider these three primary blends:

Fabric Feel and performance Compression Best application
Nylon/elastane (about 75/25) Buttery soft, cool hand-feel, strong 4-way stretch, resists pilling Medium sculpting Premium yoga leggings, sports bras, lifestyle sets
Polyester/elastane (about 80/20) Crisp, quick-drying, strong dye absorption, holds its shape High compression Printed leggings, cross-training tights, running base layers
Cotton blend fleece Warm, soft, heavier weight (320–400 GSM) Low Hoodies, joggers, athleisure outerwear

Nylon (polyamide) with elastane is the usual benchmark for premium activewear because it resists pilling from barbell knurling and keeps its recovery after repeated washing. Polyester blends are the better pick for bold all-over prints and budget-conscious ranges.

Flatlock seams: why they matter for chafing

Standard T-shirts use overlock stitching, which leaves a raised ridge of thread against the skin. Under sweat and repeated movement such as running, cycling or deadlifting, that ridge can cause chafing and irritation.

At Gloves Storm, leggings, sports bras and compression tops are assembled on 4-needle 6-thread flatlock machines (stitch type 607). A flatlock seam lies flat on both sides of the garment and stretches with the fabric, so it is far more comfortable under friction.

Seam type Feel against skin Stretch Best for
3-thread overlock Raised ridge Limited Casual T-shirts, loungewear
4-needle 6-thread flatlock (stitch type 607) Flat and smooth High (up to 150%) Leggings, sports bras, compression tops

Squat-proof testing: how we keep leggings opaque

See-through leggings are the fastest way to lose a customer. The usual causes are lightweight fabric and loose knit construction that spreads open when stretched over the glutes during deep squats or bends.

We make leggings at 240 to 280 GSM in a dense double-knit interlock, and every production lot goes through a 90-degree bend light-box opacity test during inline quality checks, before cut panels are approved. Opacity is tested, not assumed, so we describe the result as "tested for opacity" rather than a blanket guarantee.

What affects opacity What to ask your factory
Fabric weight A GSM figure in writing (240–280 GSM for high-performance leggings)
Knit density Whether it is a high-gauge, dense double-knit interlock
Colour Dark colours hide more than light ones, so always test your lightest shade (e.g. pastels or light grey)
Testing method How and when opacity is checked in production (e.g., 90-degree bend lightbox inspection)
